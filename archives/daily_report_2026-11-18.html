Ambient LLMs: Data-Efficient Supervised Fine-tuning via Temperature Matching
👥 Giannis Daras, Marios Papachristou, Adam Klivans
Collecting large-scale, diverse, and high-quality post-training datasets for autoregressive language models is challenging. Consequently, Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) is typically applied to datasets significantly smaller than those used for pre-training, which leads to overfitting and the catastrophic forgetting of the tails of the data distribution. To prevent this collapse, we introduce Ambient SFT, a principled framework for leveraging imperfect predictions to augment the fine-tuning set. In our method, a "frozen" version of the pre-trained model serves as a noisy teacher that the fine-tuned model must match at a coarse level. In particular, we require that the high-temperature versions of the conditional distributions produced by the post-training model should match the high-temperature versions of the pre-training ones. The temperature hyperparameter controls the level of trust in the pre-trained model, with the limit $T\to \infty$ reverting back to regular SFT and $T=1$ to knowledge distillation. We demonstrate that our approach outperforms standard SFT across domain adaptation, instruction-tuning, and reasoning, leading for up to $21\%$ average relative improvement to standard SFT.

What Does a Bias Head Measure? Non-Identifiability in Head-Level Bias Attribution
👥 S. M Seefat Alam
Methods that localise social bias in large language models to individual attention heads and remove it by ablation are validated by their effect on a fairness metric, never by evidence that the statistic selecting the heads measures bias. We audit this on Qwen-2.5-7B-Instruct and Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct. The head map correlates at $ρ= -0.950$ and $-0.768$ with a label-blind response-length map, apparently identifying a surface confound. An intervention that breaks the length-label coupling rejects this reading: with the prompt held fixed, response length explains 1% of the statistic's variance. What does explain it is prompt identity, at 64% of variance, in an ordering that is not monotonic in length. Within any fixed prompt the fairness label, response length and the presence of a protected-group token are collinear at $φ= 0.95$, partly by definition of the labelling rule, so no observational analysis can attribute the map to any of them, and no method in this family performs an experiment that could. The substantive claims fail independently: bias selectivity is not late-layer-concentrated on either model under either reference reading, and no head set we tested reduces the unfairness rate when ablated. Its complement, scored on the opposite label, reliably destroys refusal behaviour ($p = 2.7\times10^{-5}$), demonstrating that the pipeline detects effects at this scale.

Structure--Residual Inductive Biases for Language Model Pretraining
👥 Hai Huang, Bartosz Żuk, Rassul Magauin
Next-token prediction is relied upon to learn both predictive behavior and internal representations. We ask whether representation structure itself should be an explicit inductive bias. Motivated by the two-part coding view of Kolmogorov complexity, we study a structure--residual principle in which a representation separates structure from realization detail. We instantiate this principle in two complementary ways. Boundary Bottleneck Concept (BBC) routes information from token segments through boundary carriers while preserving local autoregressive computation. Continuous Concept (CC) regularizes representation geometry and induces a spectral decomposition into low-frequency structure and high-frequency residual. We derive coding bounds for both views and show that multiplicity-corrected boundary attention becomes exact as the within-segment path energy vanishes. Empirically, BBC and CC exhibit strikingly different depth preferences: BBC works best in early layers, whereas CC is most effective in later layers. At 300M training tokens, combining BBC and CC improves NLL by 1.44\% on GPT-2 Medium. These gains persist with training runs up to 20B tokens. Representation diagnostics show that BBC increases the relative predictive importance of boundary states, while CC reduces spectral residual energy. Together, these results suggest that structure--residual biases can improve language-model pretraining beyond next-token prediction. Code: https://github.com/haihuang-ml/structure-residual

MoE--LoRA Methods for Multilingual Adaptation: A Systematic Empirical Study
👥 Nikit Srivastava, Joel Dag, René Speck
Routed low-rank adapters offer a parameter-efficient means to improve the performance of large language models (LLMs) across tasks. Hence, it is assumed that they can also be used to improve the support of low-resource languages in LLMs, which currently serve English far better than the thousands of other languages spoken worldwide. However, this assumption has never been verified empirically under a shared multilingual evaluation protocol. We address exactly this gap by comparing eight low-rank adaptation (LoRA) methods on a frozen base model using 200 unique languages in more than 9{,}000 evaluation settings. Our results suggest that a reduced-scale search can identify viable configurations of LoRA methods but does not reliably predict their relative performance, as the top-ranking configuration is never best at scale. LoRA reliably improves language-model fit on translation data, consistently degrades reading comprehension, and benefits low-resource languages most. These findings suggest that LoRA methods should be regarded as targeted tools rather than general-purpose means for improving the multilingual capabilities of LLMs.

Mind the Gap: A Deployed Multi-Agent System for Documentation Maintenance
👥 Lorenzo Masoero
The "AI revolution" has enabled teams across the technology industry to deliver software features at unprecedented velocity. While code evolves faster than ever, customer-facing documentation often struggles to stay up to speed. As users increasingly rely on LLM-based agents that consume documentation as their primary interface, the impact of this documentation gap worsens: stale information propagates and is treated as authoritative. Signals for identifying where documentation has fallen behind already exist in abundance, both explicit (e.g., resolved support tickets revealing what documentation lacks) and implicit (e.g., code-documentation discrepancies detectable from automated loops). We deploy a multi-agent system, built around a triage agent we call "TriAgent," that continuously extracts these signals and repairs documentation drift. Over 43 days of shadow-mode operation, our system triaged 499 tickets and surfaced 92 gaps in the documentation. Using a panel of LLM judges from independent model families, 81.1% of TriAgent's verifiable assertions were correct (245 of the 302 assertions the human resolution addresses; majority of three judges); on length-comparable pairs the panel preferred TriAgent's response in 70.5% of pairs and the human engineer's in 23.2% (the rest had no judge majority). Importantly, agent- and human-sourced replies tended to be complementary, with the human often surfacing insights that were either buried within the existing documentation or missing from it entirely; our system turns each such insight into a documentation gap and, after human review, a fix. Repairing a gap measurably improved the agent's later answer: when the agent re-answered the same ticket after the repair, blinded judges preferred the post-repair answer over its own pre-repair answer 71.4% of the time (n=35). A blinded human audit confirms strong alignment on self-improvement.

Dynamic Task Allocation with Dialogue
👥 Matthew Groholski, Weiwei Gu, Nakul Gopalan
Effective multi-agent collaboration requires agents to adapt task allocation as goals and responsibilities evolve. Prior task-allocation frameworks often assume static goals, full observability, or rely on large language models (LLMs) without explicit, verifiable beliefs about task allocation or intent. These assumptions limit their applicability to real-world collaboration, where agents often lack full information about each other's actions, progress, or local environment. To support dynamic task allocation under partial observability, we introduce a communication-augmented Bayesian task-allocation framework. Our agent uses dialogue to reduce uncertainty about the environment and collaborators, while keeping decisions grounded in explicit probabilistic beliefs. We evaluate our framework in an Overcooked domain extended with dynamically arriving tasks under both full and partial object-space observability. Our communication agent consistently outperforms a vanilla LLM agent in both settings. Additionally, our framework performs comparably to a non-communicating task-allocation baseline under full observability, but substantially outperforms it under partial observability as task complexity increases by an average speed up of 0.585 and completes on average 18 timesteps faster. These results indicate that our agent is more efficient in dynamic task allocation than baselines in partially observable environments, and that communication is important as available information decreases.
