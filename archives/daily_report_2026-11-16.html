WaferTetris: Efficient Freeform Mapping on Wafer-Scale Accelerators
👥 Dayou Du, Congjie He, Andrew Boutros
Wafer-scale accelerators offer massive compute capacity through hundreds of thousands of processing elements (PEs) interconnected across a single wafer, but realizing this potential requires efficient application mapping. Their scale makes programming these systems and mapping applications onto them challenging. Existing approaches rely on template-based mappings to maintain productivity, but these templates restrict the mapping design space. In particular, they impose fixed geometric layouts over the PE grid, often leaving compute and memory resources underutilized. In this work, we present WaferTetris, a system that removes these geometric restrictions through freeform mapping. Its Tetris-like strategy flexibly packs computation across the wafer, improving PE utilization and usable memory capacity while preserving PE-task code. We develop a recurrence-based throughput model that captures branch-and-join dependencies to guide spatial parallelism, and an architecture model with per-color gates that enable existing FPGA place-and-route algorithms to enforce WSE routing constraints. We evaluate WaferTetris on Cerebras WSE-3 using LLM decoding and scientific stencil benchmarks. For LLM decoding, it achieves up to $4.42\times$ the system throughput of a template-based automatic mapping baseline on the same fabric size. Compared with the manually optimized WaferLLM, WaferTetris supports $1.8\times$ the context length for LLaMA3.1-8B (80k versus 44k), while delivering equal or higher per-user throughput at matched context lengths, up to $1.10\times$.

Can Mechanistic Interpretability Help Explain LLM Agent Failures? A Case Study on Web Element Hallucination
👥 Mohamed Aghzal, Mor Geva, Gregory J. Stein
Mechanistic Interpretability (MI) has made significant progress in explaining the internal computations of Large Language Models (LLMs); however, these advances have yet to demonstrate comparable success in explaining the decision-making processes of LLM-based agents. This paper examines the challenges MI tools face when applied to realistic agentic failures. We focus on one such failure: web agents hallucinating actions that are not available on a website. We apply a series of mechanistic analyzes to Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct as it interacts with live websites. We find that a linear probe trained on the last prompt token can predict hallucinations before generation begins (0.80 AUC) and steering along a probe direction corrects 85\% of hallucinations, but disrupts 7\% of valid actions. However, these results only provide a partial explanation of why the agent hallucinates. The probe direction is sensitive to prompt formatting, losing much of its steering effect after a minor change to how actions are represented. Additionally, circuit identification can be difficult as the computation is distributed across multiple components and positions, and the information the agent relies on varies across examples. We discuss how these observations challenge the assumptions underlying standard MI methods for circuit discovery and feature identification, and outline future directions for making these methods better suited to explain failures in LLM-based agents.

MeReader: An Offline, Privacy-Preserving, Progress-Aware AI Assistant for Narrative eBook Reading
👥 Mohammed Efaz, Udo Bub, Itilekha Podder
This paper introduces MeReader, a privacy-preserving, offline and progress-aware AI assistant designed to function as a contextual companion for narrative reading on consumer hardware. Unlike conventional Large Language Model (LLM)-based systems that process documents/books online in an all-texts-at-once type of way, thereby risking narrative spoilers, MeReader confines both retrieval and generation to the reader's distinct position in the text. The system integrates a hybrid retrieval architecture, combining vector similarity, BM25, and summary embeddings, to ensure high fidelity to the reader's current context. We evaluate the system across classical English books using both quantitative comprehension metrics and qualitative LLM-based independent judging. The results identify a quality-latency frontier, showing that mid-sized local models (2b-4b) can achieve comprehension parity with larger counterparts (5b+) while maintaining acceptable response quality. Importantly, the proposed progress-aware boundary mechanism is shown to prevent future content leakage without compromising retrieval quality and correctness. These findings suggest that a localized, privacy-centric approach can augment, rather than replace, the digital reading experience, offering a good alternative to cloud-dependent services. Therein, there remains a lack of amalgamate systems which combine progress-aware, privacy-preserving, and narrative eBook reading with AI support. It is in this very gap that the development of MeReader came to be; synthesizing said strands into an offline, book-constrained assistant focused on enhancing comprehension without compromising narrative continuity or user privacy.

Agentic induction of common data elements and information extraction from sarcoma case report abstracts
👥 Daniel Reichenpfader, Kwaku Appiagyei, Manovah Nadarajah
Common data elements (CDEs) support clinical data comparability but are usually defined manually. We investigated whether large language model agents could derive a CDE schema from sarcoma case report abstracts and apply it at scale. An agent proposed 72,909 candidate CDEs from 24,588 PubMed abstracts; agentic consolidation yielded 1,570 CDEs. We retained 53 supported by at least 50 abstracts and attempted evidence-linked extraction across the corpus. Processing succeeded for all retained CDEs in 79.2% of abstracts, including responses indicating non-mention. Two human reviewers approved 83.0% of retained CDEs for relevance, 67.9% for clarity, and 56.6% for correctness. In 100 sampled abstracts, exact agreement with the value extracted by the LLM was 94.9% among 1,116 pairs with a non-empty answer (abstract-level bootstrap 95% CI, 93.0-96.4%). Errors concentrated in anatomical and temporal fields. These findings support agent-assisted schema drafting followed by expert curation before large-scale extraction.

Do Verifier Judgments Survive Abstraction? Measuring Verification Transport for LLM Agent Evaluation
👥 Disha Sheshanarayana, Sai Linisha Reddy Goli
LLM-based verifiers are increasingly used to evaluate agent executions, but their reliability is typically measured under a single representation of the trajectory. In deployed agent systems, the same execution may be exposed through composed tools, delegation boundaries, or compacted histories, changing what execution evidence is directly observable to the verifier. It therefore remains unclear whether correct verifier judgments persist across such abstraction boundaries. We introduce Verification Transport, a reliability test that measures judgment stability under abstraction-induced changes in execution observability. We construct matched native, abstracted, and restored views of the same frozen executions while holding the task, realized trajectory, outcome, ground truth, evaluation criterion, and verifier fixed. Across four agent-evaluation benchmarks, seven verifier backbones, four verification protocols, and three abstraction boundaries, we find substantial transport failure, reaching about 31% under macro abstraction and delegation. Verification protocols exhibit distinct failure modes: direct verification more often produces incorrect verdicts, whereas evidence-grounded and deliberative protocols tend to abstain under limited observability. This suggests that lower transport failure rates do not necessarily imply greater reliability. Restoring the corresponding execution provenance recovers 85.5% of transport failures, while recovery is lower when provenance is lossy or unavailable. These results show that native-view accuracy alone is insufficient to characterize verifier reliability when execution evidence crosses abstraction boundaries.

Evaluating Human Sensitivity to Gender Biased AI-Generated Content through Human Assessment of Google's Autocomplete Function
👥 Tamara Serrano, Nikoleta Pantelidou, Raquel Montero
Large Language Models (LLMs) are increasingly present, but their effects on human users remain underexplored. We tested whether AI-attributed information influences views regarding gender-related statements, a domain where biases are pervasive. Spanish-speaking participants recruited through Prolific evaluated biased statements derived from Google's autocomplete function and human-authored, non-biased gender-related facts. Participants were either informed that some of the material was AI-generated or received no information about its origin. AI attribution did not affect reaction times or agreement judgments. This result suggests that AI-generated content does not automatically raise credibility concerns and may be evaluated with less scrutiny compared to human-produced content.
